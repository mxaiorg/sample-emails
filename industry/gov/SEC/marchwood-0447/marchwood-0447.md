Preliminary analysis from the fieldwork sample is attached. The finding that matters is this one.

Marchwood's advisory agreement for 214 of its 1,140 accounts gives the adviser, at Section 6(c), standing authority to instruct the client's custodian to withdraw or transfer funds or securities from the account, including but not limited to amounts due to Marchwood as advisory fees. In practice Marchwood has used the authority only to debit its fees, but the authority conferred is not limited to fees. So the call is productive, here is the staff's position stated plainly. Rule 206(4)-2(d)(2)(ii) defines custody to include any arrangement under which the adviser is authorized to withdraw client funds or securities maintained with a custodian upon its instruction to the custodian. That is exactly what Marchwood's advisory agreement authorizes at Section 6(c). The rule does not turn on whether the adviser could have taken more than its fee; it turns on whether the adviser can instruct the custodian to move client assets. It can.

The exception the firm is relying on is paragraph (b)(3), and it does not reach this arrangement. That exception relieves an adviser of the paragraph (a)(4) surprise examination only where its custody arises solely as a consequence of its authority to withdraw client funds to pay its advisory fee. Section 6(c) is not drafted that way. It authorizes Marchwood to instruct the custodian to withdraw or transfer funds or securities without limitation to the fee, and the staff reads the exception against the authority conferred rather than against the use the firm has so far made of it. The consequence is the surprise examination requirement in paragraph (a)(4), which Marchwood has not met for 2010.
